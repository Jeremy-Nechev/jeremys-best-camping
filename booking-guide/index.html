Booking & reservations

Recreation.gov and ReserveCalifornia both release sites on a rolling window, and the good ones go fast. Here's how the windows work, and where to go when your first choice is already gone.

How release windows work

  1. 01

    Know your window

    Most national park and California state park campgrounds release sites 6 months out. Some U.S. Forest Service campgrounds release on a shorter 5 month window. First-come, first-served sites have no window at all, you just show up.

  2. 02

    Mark the exact minute

    Both recreation.gov and ReserveCalifornia open the next day's inventory at 7:00 a.m. Pacific. For a popular weekend at a small campground, sites can be gone in seconds. Set an alarm, not a reminder.

  3. 03

    Book any date in the window, then adjust

    If your exact dates aren't open yet, grab whatever is available inside your travel window and shift it once the rest of your dates release. Cancelling and rebooking is normal, and refunds are prompt on both systems.

  4. 04

    Watch for cancellations

    Both systems show cancelled sites the moment they're released. The two best windows to check are 2 to 3 weeks out, when people finalize plans, and 24 to 48 hours out, when people cancel for weather or work.

  5. 05

    Have a backup before you book

    Pick a first-come or partially reservable campground near your target before your release day arrives. If the booking fails, you already know where to go instead.

6 months out

The standard window for national park campgrounds on recreation.gov and California state park campgrounds on ReserveCalifornia. This covers the majority of sites on this site, including Yosemite, Lake Tahoe, Sequoia & Kings Canyon, and most of the Southern California destinations.

Partial

Some campgrounds reserve part of the loop and leave the rest first-come. Princess in Sequoia National Forest, Bridalveil Creek in Yosemite, and Hanna Flat in the Big Bear area all work this way. If the reservable loop is full, drive through anyway, the first-come loop fills separately and later in the day.

First-come, first-served

No reservation, no app, no release date. Arrive early, especially on Fridays and the day before a holiday. These are the most reliable fallback when everything reservable is booked, and several are listed below.

Other places to look

National forest dispersed camping. Outside developed campgrounds, most national forest land allows free dispersed camping without a reservation. Rules vary by forest, some require a free permit and a campfire permit, and most limit stays to 14 days. Check the specific national forest's website before you go, and always follow existing fire restrictions.

Private campgrounds and Hipcamp. Private campgrounds, RV resorts, and Hipcamp listings near popular parks book up more slowly than the parks themselves and are worth a look when public land is full. Expect fewer amenities restrictions and higher prices in exchange for availability.

State parks outside the main release window. A handful of ReserveCalifornia sites at every park are held back and released 2 days before arrival specifically to cover last-minute trips. They're not guaranteed, but checking the day-of inventory two mornings before you leave is worth the five minutes.

Common questions

What time do sites actually release? 7:00 a.m. Pacific, on both recreation.gov and ReserveCalifornia, exactly however many months out the campground's window allows. Log in a few minutes early, sites can be sorted, filtered, and ready to book the second the clock turns over.

Can I book a first-come site in advance? No. That's what makes it a reliable fallback, there's no app, account, or release date to compete over, only a drive time.

How often should I check for cancellations? A few times a week is enough for most trips. In the final 2 to 3 weeks before your date, checking daily catches more of what opens up.

Is dispersed camping the same as first-come camping? No. First-come sites are still a developed campground with a numbered site, a fee, and usually a toilet and bear box. Dispersed camping is undeveloped, usually free, and has no services at all.
