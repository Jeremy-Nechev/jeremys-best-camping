Shelter

Tent

What I use and why it's held up.

Look for a freestanding double-wall design with at least a 10,000mm rainfly rating and taped seams. Size up one person from what the label says. A 4-person tent for a family of three means room for gear bags on a wet morning, which matters more than the extra half-pound.

Footprint or tarp

Cheap insurance for the tent floor.

A footprint cut to match your tent's exact dimensions, or slightly smaller, keeps rain from pooling underneath. A basic polyethylene tarp from a hardware store works just as well as a branded one; just trim it so no edges stick out past the tent to catch runoff.

Stakes

The ones that came with your tent are probably bad.

Swap the thin wire stakes that ship with most tents for heavier aluminum or steel ones. Bring a few more than you think you need; car campgrounds have rocky, root-filled ground, and it's common to bend one or two per trip.

Sleep

Sleeping bag

Rated for the coldest night you'll actually have.

Buy for the coldest night on your calendar, not the average one, and knock the rating down another 10 degrees if you sleep cold. Synthetic fill costs less and handles damp car-camping conditions better than down, which is worth the extra money mainly for backpacking.

Sleeping pad

The single biggest upgrade to how you sleep outside.

An insulated pad with an R-value of 4 or higher blocks the cold coming up from the ground, which matters more than the bag on top of you most nights. For car camping, a thicker self-inflating or air pad is worth the extra bulk since you're not carrying it far.

Pillow

An inflatable one is worth the eight ounces.

A small inflatable camp pillow packs down to nothing and beats a rolled-up jacket every time. Look for one with a brushed fabric top so it doesn't slide off the pad in the middle of the night.

Kitchen

Two-burner stove

Enough to cook a real dinner.

A propane two-burner stove with independent flame control on each side lets you simmer a sauce on one burner while boiling water on the other. Windscreens built into the sides help a lot at exposed, breezy campgrounds.

Cooler

How long it actually holds ice.

A rotomolded hard cooler holds ice for four or five days in warm weather, versus a day or two for a basic cooler, which is the difference on a longer trip. Pack it with block ice on the bottom and drinks on top, and keep it in shade; that alone extends ice life more than any single feature.

Water storage

For campgrounds without a spigot.

A collapsible 5- to 7-gallon jug with a spigot covers cooking and dish-washing for a weekend without a water tap nearby. Check each campground's water situation before you go; several of the sites on this list, especially in the desert, have none at all.

Everything else

Headlamp

One per person, always.

A headlamp with at least 200 lumens and a red-light mode covers everything from cooking after dark to a nighttime bathroom trip without ruining your night vision. Pack spare batteries; cold nights drain them faster than you expect.

Camp chair

You'll sit in it more than you expect.

Look for a chair with a steel frame, a cupholder, and a weight rating comfortably above your own. The cheap aluminum ones are lighter to carry but rarely survive more than a season or two of real use.

First aid kit

What I add to the store-bought one.

A basic store-bought kit covers cuts and blisters, but add moleskin, tweezers for splinters, any personal medications, and a printed list of emergency numbers for the area, since cell service is spotty at most of these campgrounds. Check and restock it once a year.
